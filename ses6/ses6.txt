Next, RMSSE, the square root of MSE. It is more intuitive because the unit matches Y. If Y is in dollars, RMSSE is in dollars. MSE would be dollars squared. In our example, RMS equals the square roo of 37.7, which is approximately 6.1. Next, M AE, mean absolute error, the average of absolute errors. Unlike MSE, large errors contribute proportionally to size, not size squared, making MAE less sensitive to outliers. Think of MAE as the average mistake your model makes. In our example, MAE is 1/3 of the absolute value of 10 plus the absolute value of 2 plus the absolute value of -3 which equals 5. Finally, R 2, the coefficient of determination. R 2 indicates fit quality with a maximum of 1. The formula is 1 minus the sum of squared errors over the sum of squared deviations from the mean. R 2 compares the model against a dumb baseline that always predicts the mean. In our example, the mean of y is 10 + 9 + 5 / 3, which equals 8. Plugging in, r 2 is approximately -7.07, meaning the model is worse than predicting the mean. R 2 is as outlier sensitive as MSE since it uses squared errors. Here's a practical summary of the regression metrics. MSE and RMSSE penalize large errors heavily. Use them when a single big miss is very costly. such as demand forecasting or investment forecasting. Smaller is better. M AE evaluates average prediction quality and is less sensitive to outliers. Use it when occasional large errors can be offset by accurate on average predictions. Smaller is better. R 2 is 1.0 for perfect predictions, 0.0 for the mean baseline, and negative for worse than baseline. Use it for scaleindependent fit comparison. Closer to one is better.
